Methods and fields of application

A catalogue of every method implemented in the fatigue module: the governing equation, the inputs it consumes, the situations in which it is a reasonable engineering choice, and the situations in which it is not. Parameter-level detail is in Parameters and status codes; numerical behaviour and worked examples are in Numerics.

Scope of the guarantees. The module verifies that equations are evaluated correctly and that units, provenance and ranges are consistent. It does not validate that a method is appropriate for a given material, component or load. Every method below is an empirical model; its accuracy is bounded by the quality of the material data and by how well the assumptions hold. No safety factor, statistical scatter, size/surface/notch factor or environment effect is applied anywhere.

2. Cycle counting: ASTM E1049 Rainflow

Input: a StressHistory (ordered tensor samples with physical time) and a unit direction \(n\) (StressProjection). Output: a list of LoadCycle with range, mean and count weight.

\[ s_k=n^\top\boldsymbol\sigma_k\,n=\sum_{i,j}n_i\,\sigma_{k,ij}\,n_j,\qquad \Delta s=|s_b-s_a|,\qquad s_m=\tfrac12(s_a+s_b). \]
  1. Plateau merge. Consecutive samples with identical projected value collapse into one point, retaining the last physical occurrence.
  2. Turning points. The first and last points are always kept; an interior point is kept only if the slope changes sign.
  3. Three-point stack. Points are pushed one at a time. While the stack holds at least three points, let \(X=|s_{n-1}-s_{n}|\) (newest range) and \(Y=|s_{n-2}-s_{n-1}|\) (previous range). If \(X<Y\) stop; otherwise a cycle of range \(Y\) is extracted: if \(Y\) involves the oldest stack point it is counted as a half cycle (weight 0.5) and the oldest point is dropped, else as a full cycle (weight 1) and the two middle points are removed.
  4. Residue. What remains on the stack is counted as consecutive half cycles (weight 0.5).

Applicability. Any signal for which a scalar can be justified. The count is independent of time scale (no rate effects) and of the sampling density between turning points. Limitations. The history is treated as a single block that is not assumed to repeat: half cycles from the residue are reported as such. If the block repeats, concatenate it with itself or re-order it so that it starts at the largest peak to merge residue half cycles into full cycles. A strictly monotone history produces a single half cycle.

Rainflow is applied to the projected value only. Sign changes of the projection that are due to rotating principal axes are therefore counted as scalar cycles, which can under- or over-estimate damage for non-proportional loading.

3. S–N curves

A curve consists of one or more temperature slices; each slice holds points \((\sigma_{a,i},N_i)\) with strictly increasing \(\sigma_a\) and strictly decreasing \(N\). Interpolation is linear in \((\log_{10}\sigma_a,\log_{10}N)\) (Basquin-type straight segments), and linear in temperature on \(\log_{10}N\). No extrapolation in amplitude or temperature is performed: an amplitude outside the common range of the bracketing slices is a hard failure (status CurveEvaluationFailed). In particular amplitudes below the lowest tabulated point do not return an infinite life; if the S–N data have a fatigue limit, extend the table with a low-amplitude point at a very large life (for example \(10^{12}\) cycles). That is a modelling decision that you make explicitly; the library never assumes an endurance limit.

Consequence. A tension-side mean-stress correction increases the equivalent amplitude and can push it beyond the highest tabulated point; that cycle then makes the whole evaluation fail instead of silently contributing damage. Tabulate the curve up to the highest amplitude you expect after correction.

4. Mean-stress corrections for S–N use

All strengths use the same stress unit as the curve. Let \(\sigma_a\) be the amplitude, \(\sigma_m\) the mean and \(\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_m+\sigma_a\).

MethodEquivalent amplitude \(\sigma_{a,c}\)Typical useRejected when
None\(\sigma_a\)Fully reversed S–N data or a mean-stress-insensitive material/regime.—
Goodman\(\sigma_a/(1-\sigma_m/S_u)\)Conservative choice for brittle materials and general tension means.\(S_u\) missing, \(\sigma_m\ge S_u\)
Gerber\(\sigma_a/(1-(\sigma_m/S_u)^2)\)Ductile metals under tension means; symmetric in \(\sigma_m\) so it raises the amplitude for compressive means, which is non-conservative in reality (see table below).\(S_u\) missing, \(|\sigma_m|\ge S_u\)
Soderberg\(\sigma_a/(1-\sigma_m/S_y)\)Most conservative; design against yielding.\(S_y\) missing, \(\sigma_m\ge S_y\)
Morrow\(\sigma_a/(1-\sigma_m/S_f)\)Ductile steels where the true fracture strength \(S_f\) is known; less conservative than Goodman.\(S_f\) missing, \(\sigma_m\ge S_f\)
SWT (S–N form)\(\sqrt{\sigma_{\max}\,\sigma_a}\)One-parameter form without strengths; reduces to \(\sigma_a\) at \(R=-1\).\(\sigma_{\max}\le0\)

Compressive means. Goodman, Soderberg and Morrow reduce the equivalent amplitude when \(\sigma_m<0\), i.e. they credit compressive mean stress; Gerber increases it. 5. Linear damage accumulation (Palmgren–Miner)

\[ D=\sum_i\frac{n_i}{N_i},\qquad L_{\rm block}=\begin{cases}1/D&D>0\\+\infty&D=0.\end{cases} \]

Compensated (Kahan) summation is used, so the result does not depend on cycle order beyond rounding. Applicability: a first-order lifetime estimate for variable-amplitude loading when load sequence effects are secondary. Not captured: sequence effects (high–low versus low–high), overload retardation, amplitude-dependent critical damage (\(D_{\rm crit}\neq1\)), scatter. For a design check compare \(D\) with an allowable value below 1 chosen according to your applicable standard; the module reports \(D\) and \(1/D\) only.

6. Strain-life (ε–N) relations

All three use the Coffin–Manson–Basquin (CMB) elastic and plastic strain lines. With \(2N_f\) reversals, strain amplitude \(\varepsilon_a\), Young's modulus \(E\) and constants \(\sigma_f',b,\varepsilon_f',c\) (\(b,c<0\)):

\[ \varepsilon_a=\frac{\sigma_f'}{E}(2N_f)^b+\varepsilon_f'(2N_f)^c. \]
ModelEquation solved for \(2N_f\)Fields of applicationRequirements
CMB\(\varepsilon_a=\dfrac{\sigma_f'}{E}(2N_f)^b+\varepsilon_f'(2N_f)^c\)Fully reversed or mean-stress-insensitive cycles; short lives where mean stress relaxes.—
Morrow\(\varepsilon_a=\dfrac{\sigma_f'-\sigma_m}{E}(2N_f)^b+\varepsilon_f'(2N_f)^c\)Tension or compression means in ductile steels; mean affects the elastic term only, so it matters mostly for long lives (\(N_f\gtrsim N_t\)).\(\sigma_f'-\sigma_m>0\) for every cycle
SWT\(\sigma_{\max}\varepsilon_a=\dfrac{\sigma_f'^2}{E}(2N_f)^{2b}+\sigma_f'\varepsilon_f'(2N_f)^{b+c}\)Materials with high mean-stress sensitivity, e.g. many aluminium alloys and high-strength steels; no extra constant.\(\sigma_{\max}>0\); cycles with \(\sigma_{\max}\le0\) are rejected

The transition life \(2N_t=(\varepsilon_f'E/\sigma_f')^{1/(b-c)}\) separates plastic-dominated (short) from elastic-dominated (long) behaviour; for the example material of the numerics page it is \(2N_t\approx1.14\times10^4\) reversals. Because the relation is a sum of two decreasing power laws, it is strictly monotone and the inverse is unique; see Numerics for the solver.

7. Local elastic–plastic correction at notches

From an elastic FE stress amplitude \(\sigma_e\) at a notch root the local cyclic stress \(\sigma\) and strain \(\varepsilon\) are obtained with the cyclic Ramberg–Osgood curve \(\varepsilon=\sigma/E+(\sigma/K')^{1/n'}\) and one of two energy-type rules:

RuleEquationField of application
Neuber\(\sigma\,\varepsilon=\sigma_e^2/E\)Plane-stress, thin sections, small plastic zones. Tends to over-predict local strain (conservative) as plasticity grows.
Glinka (ESED)\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\varepsilon}\!\sigma\,d\varepsilon=\frac{\sigma^2}{2E}+\frac{\sigma\,\varepsilon_p}{1+n'}=\frac{\sigma_e^2}{2E}\)Notched components under plane strain or moderate plasticity; gives a smaller strain than Neuber in every case tested (\(n'<1\)); less conservative.

Both rules are applied to each cycle independently, using the amplitude (half range) and keeping the elastic mean stress: the maximum stress passed on to Morrow/SWT is \(\sigma_{m,e}+\sigma\). Limitations. The rules assume proportional, uniaxial stress at the root and a stabilised cyclic curve; they do not model the evolution of the mean stress by plastic shake-down (\(\sigma_m\) is not relaxed), hysteresis-loop memory (every cycle is converted from its own amplitude, independently) or multiaxial plasticity. The plastic zone must be small compared with the notch and section dimensions; when the local-to-elastic strain ratio exceeds roughly 2–3 an elastic–plastic FE solution is recommended.

The cyclic parameters must satisfy \(0<n'\le1\), \(K'>0\), \(E>0\). In the elastic limit both rules give \(\varepsilon\to\sigma_e/E\). Results for the example material (\(E=210\,000\) MPa, \(K'=1000\) MPa, \(n'=0.15\)), computed with the library; \(\sigma\) is the local stress amplitude:

The gap between the two rules widens quickly with plasticity: at \(\sigma_e=600\) MPa Neuber predicts 26% more strain than Glinka, and the predicted CMB life differs by a factor of about 2.2 (3767 versus 8196 cycles in the example). The choice of rule is therefore a first-order modelling decision in the low-cycle regime and should be made against test data for the component class.

8. Wang–Brown multiaxial strain-life

For non-proportional strain paths the damage parameter combines the maximum shear strain amplitude \(\gamma_{\max}/2\) of the cycle with the normal-strain excursion \(\Delta\varepsilon_n\) on the plane of maximum shear (Wang & Brown, 1993):

\[ \frac{\Delta\gamma_{\max}}{2}+S\,\Delta\varepsilon_n=C_1\frac{\sigma_f'}{E}(2N_f)^b+C_2\,\varepsilon_f'(2N_f)^c,\] \[ C_1=1+\nu_e+(1-\nu_e)S,\qquad C_2=1+\nu_p+(1-\nu_p)S. \]

Here \(S\) is a material constant measuring sensitivity to non-proportional hardening/additional cracking (calibrated from combined tension–torsion tests; \(S=0\) recovers a pure shear-strain criterion), \(\nu_e\) and \(\nu_p\) are the elastic and plastic Poisson ratios. In WangBrownDamageModel the left-hand side is built from maximumShearStrainAmplitude + \(S\)·normalStrainExcursion and the equation is inverted for \(2N_f\) with the same safeguarded Newton solver as the strain-life models. Required: \(-1<\nu_e,\nu_p<0.5\), \(S\ge0\), non-negative strain measures.

Supporting building block. WangBrownRelativeStrain evaluates the scalar measure

\[ \Delta\varepsilon_{\rm rel}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_A,\boldsymbol\varepsilon_B;\nu)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2\,(1+\nu)}\sqrt{(\Delta\varepsilon_x-\Delta\varepsilon_y)^2+(\Delta\varepsilon_x-\Delta\varepsilon_z)^2+(\Delta\varepsilon_y-\Delta\varepsilon_z)^2+6(\Delta\varepsilon_{xy}^2+\Delta\varepsilon_{xz}^2+\Delta\varepsilon_{yz}^2)} \]

between two strain tensors (tensor shear components), together with helpers that locate the maximum-magnitude or maximum-relative strain index along a history. Status. The damage model and the relative-strain helpers are implemented and unit-tested, but there is no end-to-end orchestration that automatically extracts \(\gamma_{\max}\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon_n\) per cycle from a FEM tensor history: that step (path-cycle counting and plane search) is the caller's responsibility, through the multiaxial interface described on the multiaxial page. The Wang–Brown constants \(S,\nu_e,\nu_p\) have no defaults and must come from calibration data.
